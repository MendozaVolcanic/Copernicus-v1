Earth Observation data are playing an increasingly central role in volcanology, enabling high-resolution 
assessments of the timing, magnitude, and explosivity of eruptive events. A comprehensive suite 
of satellite-derived products is provided here, documenting the February 2025 eruption of Mt. Etna 
(Italy), the first eruption fully monitored also by the third-generation Meteosat satellite, providing 
unprecedented mid-infrared spatial and temporal resolution. Daily Planet’s acquisitions enabled 
consistent monitoring of lava flow evolution, while a post-eruptive Pléiades triplet allowed for the 
development of an updated digital surface model and precise estimation of the deposits thickness. 
The dataset includes: time averaged discharge rates (TADR) from MODIS, SEVIRI, VIIRS, and FCI 
sensors; lava flow areal expansion from Skysat and PlanetScope imagery; deposit thickness from DSMs 
differencing; SO2 mass flux from TROPOMI; a 5 m-resolution DSM from Pléiades imagery (March 6th, 
2025). Combined analysis of SO2 emissions and TADR improves understanding of magma supply and 
eruptive output. Furthermore, the products provided offer critical data for analyzing multi-temporal 
morphological changes and hazard assessment studies.
Background & Summary
In February 2025, a new eruptive phase both effusive and explosive started on Mt Etna (Italy). The explosive-type 
activity began on February 6th, 2025 with a modest and episodic Strombolian activity produced by a vent located 
in the western sector of the South-East Crater (SEC), while on February 8th, a new effusive phase began, fed by 
an eruptive fissure opened at the base of the Bocca Nuova (BN) crater at an elevation of ~3,050 meters above 
sea level (a.s.l.). The lava flow advanced rather rapidly southwestward over a snow-covered surface, reaching a 
quote of ~2,150 meters a.s.l. on February 11th and 1,960 meters a.s.l. on February 13th. The interaction between 
the lava and the snowpack caused sudden explosions projecting incandescent fragments of lava up to several 
tens of meters away. Weak explosive activity was also detected from a vent located between the SEC and the BN 
(Fig. 1). The explosive activity produced mild volcanic ash emissions, which were rapidly dispersed by the winds 
and deposited in the immediate proximity of the volcano summit. On February 12th, 14th and 16th, the analysis 
of images from the INGV-OE video surveillance system, together with field observations, showed an increase 
in volcanic ash emission with an eruptive column not exceeding 5,500 meters a.s.l. After a reduction in effusive 
activity at the vents below the BN and interruption of the SEC’s explosive activity, lava emission resumed from 
February 22th, accompanied by the return of Strombolian activity at the SEC on February 23th. This activity 
continued until the morning of the 25th, when Strombolian activity at the SEC again came to a halt and the 
effusive rate decreased significantly. In the evening of February 27th, the SEC reactivated with Strombolian 
explosions, followed by the resumption of effusive activity from the vent at the base of BN.
A small lava overflow was also observed from the southern rim of SEC on February 24th and 28th. Moreover, 
the presence of fissures, cracks and a new pit crater on the southern flank of the BN were detected, with an inter-
mittent emission of steam.
The effusive activity beneath the BN generated multiple lava branches, which expanded on the upper south-
western flank, thus enlarging the upper part of the lava field that had been forming since February 8th (Fig. 1). In 
the evening of February 28th, explosive activity at SEC gradually decreased and came to a halt during the night.

This eruption is a magnificent example of how a multitude of information from satellite imagery has made it 
possible to reconstruct and quantify in real time the evolution of the different phases of eruptive activity. Here 
we provide the different products we retrieved, including the expansion of the lava field, the average eruptive 
rates and volumes emitted, as well as the thickness of volcanic deposits (Table 1).
A brief description of the main characteristics of the different satellite imagery used (Table 1) is reported 
below.
The Spinning Enhanced Visible and Infrared Imager (SEVIRI) is the primary payload of the Meteosat Second 
Generation geostationary satellites. The mission, which was launched in 2002, acquires data on 11 spectral chan-
nels at 3 km spatial resolution at nadir, with an additional panchromatic high-resolution channel (1 km spatial 
resolution at nadir). The primary acquisition service provides a full disk image of the Earth every 15 minutes, 
with a sub-satellite longitude of 0°. A Rapid Scan Service (RSS) is also available, that only scans the upper third 
of the full disk and provides an image every 5 minutes. The RSS satellite is typically located at 9.5° longitude east; 
8 of the 11 standard channels are in the infrared and one of these (Channel 4, 3.9 µm central wavelength) can be 
used to monitor and characterize high temperature events, such as forest fires and volcanic eruptions, especially 
paired with Channel 10 (10.8 µm central wavelength).
The Flexible Combined Imager (FCI) is the successor of the SEVIRI sensor onboard the Meteosat Third 
Generation geostationary satellites. The mission was launched in December 2023 and acquires data over 16 
spectral channels with a spatial resolution that varies from 0.5 km to 2 km at nadir. The infrared channels at 
3.8 µm and 10.5 µm are particularly important for volcanic monitoring, as they are captured in High Resolution 
mode with a spatial resolution of 1 km at nadir and a temporal frequency of 10 minutes.
The Visible Infrared Imaging Radiometer Suite (VIIRS) instrument observes and collects global satellite obser-
vations that span the visible and infrared wavelengths across land, ocean, and atmosphere. It has 22 channels 
ranging from 0.41 μm to 12.01 μm. Five of these channels are high-resolution image bands or I-bands (375 m 
spatial resolution), and sixteen serve as moderate-resolution bands or M-bands (750 m spatial resolution). It is 
mounted on the NOAA-20 and NOAA-21 weather satellites of the joint Suomi-NPP mission.

and in 2002 with Aqua spacecrafts. It has a viewing swath width of 2,330 km and views the entire surface of the

surface temperature and also the high temperatures associated with volcanic eruptions.
SkySat is a constellation of ~15 high-resolution Earth imaging satellites able to image revisit any location on 
Earth up to 10 times daily (a daily collection capacity of 4,000 km2/day.) SkySat images are approximately 50 
centimeters per pixel resolution and are acquired in the RGB bands plus a near infrared channel. They are owned 
and operated by the commercial company Planet Labs1.
PlanetScope is a constellation of approximately 130 satellites, able to image the entire land surface of the 
Earth every day (a daily collection capacity of 200 million km2/day) at approximately 3 meters per pixel reso-
lution. Since the first launch in 2014, three PlanetScope instrument types have been released. The last one, the 
SuperDove, acquires data in the red, green, blue, near infrared, as well as a new red edge, green I, coastal blue, 
and yellow channels. Planescope is owned and operated by the commercial company Planet Labs1.
The Pléiades satellite constellation (Pléiades 1 A and 1B, launched respectively in 2011 and 2012) delivers 
very high resolution optical imagery with a daily revisit capability for any point on Earth. The sensor acquires 
data in tri-stereo configuration allowing for the three-dimensional reconstruction of the Earth surface for a 
range of remote sensing applications in the fields of cartography, agriculture, forestry, hydrology and geological 
prospecting.
TROPOspheric Monitoring Instrument (TROPOMI), which was launched in October 2017 aboard the ESA 
and Copernicus Sentinel-5 Precursor satellite, is specifically designed for atmospheric observations and provides 
a spatial resolution of 3.5 × 5.5 km2 per pixel on the ground. The operational algorithm for SO2 detection on 
TROPOMI uses the differential optical absorption spectroscopy (DOAS) method2. The TROPOMI instrument 
captures data across four spectral regions (i.e., ultraviolet, visible, near-infrared, and short-wave infrared) ena-
bling the detection of SO2 along with several other gases (O3, NO2, CO, CH4, and HCHO) and aerosols3.
Methods
Satellite data were promptly elaborated to monitor the eruptive activity of Etna during the 2025 February erup-
tion. In particular, we processed SEVIRI, MODIS, VIIRS and FCI data using the CL-HOTSAT system to esti-
mate the effusion rate and hence the lava flow volume, providing quantitative information about the different 
phases of the eruption. Moreover, we retrieved the thickness distribution of volcanic deposits by differencing 
successive digital topographies obtained by Pléiades imagery using the MicMac software. We also extracted the 
evolution of the lava flow field using Skysat and PlanetScope data, and estimated the sulfur dioxide total mass on 
Sentinel-5P TROPOMI imagery using a new clustering approach implemented in Google Earth Engine.
Details about the algorithms used, data processing and analyses, are reported below.
Estimation of the time-averaged discharge rate and erupted volumes. 
CL-HOTSAT is a satellite 
thermal monitoring system specifically designed for Mt Etna4, but successfully applied also to other volcanic 
areas5,6. It ingests multi-spectral satellite data acquired by SEVIRI, MODIS and VIIRS sensors. CL-HOTSAT 
was recently specialized to process FCI data. For this sensor, we used the georeferencing datasets present in the 
NetCDF files, which provide the per row and per column satellite view angle in radians, limited to the specific 
chunk presented in the file. Once georeferencing information is converted into a form digestible by standard GIS 
software, and radiance information is obtained by calibrating the digital numbers, the script writes to disk a new 
raster, georeferenced through selected ground control points. This raster can be fed to the existing CL-HOTSAT 
system for further processing. The system uses the difference between the Middle Infrared and the Thermal 
Infrared bands and provides hotspot location in an automatic fashion. In the case of FCI we used the IR3.8 and 
the IR 10.5 bands. The hotspot algorithm is contextual and employs the well-known position of the volcanoes 
searching a threshold in a portion of the image considered “non volcanic”. For each hotspot pixel, the radiant heat 
flux is computed by following the MIR radiance approach7. This information can be converted into Time-Average 
Discharge Rate (TADR, i.e. the effusion rate averaged over given periods), from which the erupted volume is 
obtained by integration, in case of effusive eruptions.
Development of digital surface models and 3D mapping of volcanic deposits. 
To estimate the 
thickness distribution of the deposit and the lava volume emitted during the February 2025 Etna eruption, we 
used a topographic approach relying on the difference of Pléiades-derived DSMs. Pléiades images were processed 
using the free and open source MicMac photogrammetric library8. The pre-eruptive DSM was derived from a 
Pléaides triplet acquired on December 27th, 2023, while the post-eruptive DSM was obtained from the July 15th, 
2024 and the March 6th, 2025, acquisitions. Since the March 6th acquisition was partially affected by cloud cover-
age, a fusion algorithm was applied to build a smoother homogeneous post-eruptive product9.
In order to reconstruct the 3D distribution of volcanic deposits minimizing the errors due to misalignments, 
we co-registered10 and then subtracted the two DSMs. The volume was calculated by integrating the thickness 
distribution over the area covered by the deposits. The uncertainty was quantified by multiplying the area and 
the standard deviation of terrain residuals outside of the deposits.
Tracking of lava flow emplacement. 
To ensure a detailed periodic mapping of lava flow advancement, we 
applied a semi-automatic thresholding and change detection algorithm, which has been successfully used during 
recent effusive eruptions at Etna11,12. First, Skysat and Planetscope images were imported into the GIS (specifi-
cally GRASS and QGIS) and RGB composites (using the RED, GREEN and near infrared spectral bands) were 
created to highlight the characteristics of the lava and detect changes associated with the emplacement of new 
lava among subsequent acquisitions. In this way, we obtained the boundaries of the lava flow on 9, 11, 12, 17, 21,

Estimation of the sulfur dioxide total mass. 
Sulfur dioxide (SO2) total mass was calculated on 
Sentinel-5P TROPOMI imagery, applying two different clustering techniques on the open-source Google Earth 
Engine computing platform. For the analysis, we exploited the offline Level 3 product at 15 km, applying a Simple 
Non-Iterative Clustering (SNIC) algorithm segmentation and then a k-means unsupervised classifier, followed by 
the application of a threshold for highlighting SO2-contaminated pixels13. Specifically, we calculated the density 
for each of the classes derived from the previous clustering. The density is defined as the ratio between the total 
mass value of sulfur dioxide (obtained by summing the SO2 values of each pixel within the class) and the relative 
number of pixels contained in that class. Ultimately, we selected only those classes with a density higher than 
0.00028 mol/m2, which provided an optimal value for the eruptive events occurring at Etna. This approach for 
object-based image analysis is designed to more accurately characterize sulfur dioxide, avoiding interference from 
noise that could derive, for example, from the sensor instrumentation onboard the satellites or from the lighting 
and atmospheric conditions14.
Data Records
The data record associated with this work consists of different products (Fig. 2), whose details about format and 
external links are provided below. All data are shared under the CC BY 4.0 use license and available at the link 
https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.28759586.
Time-averaged discharge rate and erupted volumes. 
We provide the TADR and volumes (with 
related uncertainties) extracted from SEVIRI, MODIS, FCI and VIIRS imagery from 8 February to 2 March 2025, 
which have been elaborated by the CL-HOTSAT system. We also provide location in terms of latitude and longi-
tude for the hotspot pixels for each sensor. These estimates are supplied as an xlsx file with a tab for each sensor 
and one for the cumulative volumes (TADR&Volumes.xlsx). Date is expressed in Greenwich Mean Time (GMT) 
as dd/mm/yyyy hh.mm; minimum, mean and maximum value for TADR are expressed in m3/s; minimum, mean 
and maximum value for cumulative volumes in m3; latitude and longitude are expressed in decimal degrees.
Digital surface models and 3D mapping of volcanic deposits. 
We provide the most updated digital 
topography of Etna, obtained from Pléiades imagery acquired on March 6th, 2025 (Etna_DSM_25_5m.tif). The 
DSM covers an area of about 95 km2, including the summit craters of Mt Etna and the Valle del Bove. It is pro-
vided as a Geotiff, with a spatial resolution of 5 meters. Coordinates refer to the WGS-84 ellipsoid; the map pro-
jection is UTM (Zone 33 North). The 3D mapping of volcanic deposits, which shows the thickness distribution of 
the final lava flow field, is provided as GEOTIFF (lava_flow_thickness.tiff), with elevations in meters relative to 
the WGS-84 UTM 33 North Coordinate System (EPSG: 32633).
Lava flow emplacement. 
The actual fields of the lava flow emplaced during the February 2025 Etna erup-
tion were retrieved for the following days: 9, 11, 12, 17, 21, 24, 26 February and 4 March. They are provided as 
vector shapefiles into the WGS084 UTM 33 North Coordinate System (EPSG: 32633), showing the areal develop-
ment and the advancement of the lava front. The shapefile’s attribute table includes fields called “area” and “perim-
eter” that contain the measurements of each map feature (lava_flow_fields.zip). The areas show an increment 
from 0.054 to ~0.94 km2, which represents the total lava coverage.
Sulfur dioxide total mass. 
The retrieval of sulfur dioxide is provided in a csv file with the date expressed 
as dd/mm/yyyy and the minimum, mean and maximum total mass values in ktons (SO2_Total Mass.csv). Some 
data are marked as “not available” (“N.A.”), since they do not exceed the measurable detection limit by TROPOMI 
for SO2, in terms of the lowest concentration that can be reliably detected (equal to 0.0124 ton).
Daily maps of pixels containing SO2 are also provided in GeoTiff format (SO2_maps.zip), which are georef-
erenced to the WGS-84 UTM 33 North coordinate system (EPSG: 32633).
Technical Validation
The height accuracy of the March 6th, 2025 DSM was estimated by using GPS Ground Control Points available 
on Etna volcano15. We performed a comparison between these points and the elevations of the DSM (excluding 
those falling inside volcanic deposits), finding residuals ranging between −3.6 and 2.8 m, with a mean value of 
0.02 m and a standard deviation of 1.61 m, which represents the vertical accuracy of the DSM.
The DSM difference provides an estimation of the bulk lava volume, i.e. by including the vesicularity of the 
lava and possible cavities/tunnels, while CL-HOTSAT provides lava volume by integrating the TADR obtained 
from thermal multispectral images, i.e. based on the radiating portion of the volcanic deposits. By comparing 
the bulk volume from DSM difference (5.05 ± 0.9 × 106 m3) and the average volume from multispectral data 
(5.37 ± 0.8 × 106 m3), we found a difference of about 320,000 m3, which is well within the measurement errors7. 
The validation of the average volume from multispectral data was also done considering the estimates performed 
using drones, thermal cameras and field observations (INGV-OE, 2025a-d). These suggested an average volume 
of 0.25 × 106 m3 on February 11th, 2.1 × 106 m3 on February 16th, 3.5 × 106 m3 on February 24th and 4.5 × 106 
m3 on March 2nd, with an uncertainty of 40%. Considering our satellite-derived estimates at 12:00 UTC of the 
same days, we obtain average volumes of 0.57 × 106 m3 on February 11th, 2.05 × 106 m3 on February 16th, 4.61 
× 106 m3 on February 24th and 5.37 × 106 m3 on March 2nd.

The trend of the TADR estimated by CL-HOTSAT for the four different sensors is coherent and in line with 
the areal expansion of the lava flow field, with the maximum length reached just after the first TADR peak of 
February 16th. This was also confirmed by the field measurements made by the INGV personnel, who followed 
the advancement of the lava field and the minimum elevation reached16–18.
The SO2 mass flux is also in agreement with the TADR and in accordance with the SO2 measured from the 
ground, which both present two peaks towards higher values on February 20th and 26th19.
The lava flow emplacement was validated using independent measurements obtained from drones, thermal 
cameras and field observations16–19. In particular, we considered the maximum length of the main branch, the 
altitude reached by the front and the area covered by the lava. On February 11th, 16th, and 24th (extending to 
March 2nd), INGV-OE documented progressive lava flow lengths of 2.6 km, 4.2 km, and a peak of 4.3 km —
reaching elevations of 2150, 1860, and 1840 m a.s.l.— with corresponding flow areas expanding from 1.3 × 105 
m2 to 3 × 105 m2 and eventually stabilizing at 7 × 105 m2. Our satellite-derived measurements indicate compa-
rable or slightly greater flow lengths of 2.7 km (2160 m a.s.l.), 4.2 km (1860 m a.s.l.), and 4.3 km (1850 m a.s.l.),
